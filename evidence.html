Evidence Review Summary

Cobalt reviewed published research on each element of Let's Talk's theory of change, rating how well the evidence supports it, using the tiers defined at the end of this appendix, and how directly that evidence applies to Let's Talk. The review covered syntheses, meta-analyses and individual studies, and searched for null results and harms as well as positive findings.

Overall assessment

There is no positive causal evidence yet for Let's Talk as a whole. The one independent trial found no significant effect on MAP for the randomized sample; significant gains appear only among students teachers chose to use OKO, which WestEd treats as non-causal. Outside research is strongest for structured cooperative learning in math, especially when students must reach consensus while remaining individually accountable, which is what the solve-alone-then-agree design builds in. But two large trials of a structured cooperative math program found no effect, which the US evaluators attributed to weak implementation. OKO's score rose but did not predict MAP, and the closest comparable study found that an AI agent improved how students talked but not what they learned.

What the evidence suggests for measurement

  • Measure how sessions run, not only how long: whether solo-then-agree happens as designed, and how talk is spread within groups (Priority 2).
  • Treat participation by subgroup as a safety check. Research on single-answer group tasks finds that higher-status students can dominate, and speech recognition is less accurate for some speakers (Priorities 1 and 2).
  • Use behavioral counts, rather than the current score, as the link to growth until the redefined scores have reliability evidence (Priorities 1 and 3).
  • Avoid comparing users with non-users. In the trial, teacher-selected users started well ahead of control students; compare students within the same classroom or teacher instead (Priority 3).
  • Power any new causal study for the effects the trial actually observed across the randomized sample, not the user subgroup's (Next Steps).

Evidence tiers

Randomized trials and quasi-experimental studies count equally, provided a quasi-experimental study shows the groups were equivalent at baseline and attrition is acceptable.

TierCriteria
Very strongTwo or more well-conducted randomized or quasi-experimental studies with positive effects, at least one independent of the developer and no contrary evidence of comparable quality; or a WWC positive rating; or ESSA Tier I or II validated by a credible third party.
StrongOne well-conducted randomized or quasi-experimental study with a positive effect, or a meta-analysis in which the benefit holds among studies whose designs permit causal inference.
CorrelationalA meta-analysis or multiple studies showing consistent associations between the element and a positive outcome.
Rationale onlyA logic model or evidence for the underlying mechanism, but no outcome evidence for the practice itself (roughly ESSA Tier IV).
No evidence foundA search was done and found nothing credible. Not reviewed means no search was done.

Inconsistent means studies of comparable quality disagree. Ratings with two parts apply to different aspects of the same element.
